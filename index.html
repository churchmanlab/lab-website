Department of Genetics · Harvard Medical School

From the nucleus to mitochondria, we study the many layers of gene regulation.

A cell makes thousands of mRNAs every minute and controls every one of them — where it is made, how it is processed, how long it survives, and whether it is ever translated. We build quantitative methods that make those steps measurable, in both the nuclear and the mitochondrial genome.

Recent work

Three things we have learned lately

How RNA flows through the cell

From synthesis and chromatin release to nuclear export, ribosome engagement and eventual degradation, we quantified the lives of human transcripts genome-wide — and found that hundreds of genes are degraded in the nucleus and never exported at all.

Ietswaart et al., Molecular Cell 2024

How this fits together →

Single-molecule views of transcription and chromatin

Long-read chromatin fiber sequencing lets us see RNA polymerases in their native chromatin context along 30 kb fibers, revealing direct coordination — and anti-coordination — between neighbouring genes, enhancers and insulators.

Tullius et al., Molecular Cell 2024

How this fits together →

How mitochondrial DNA is packaged

Unlike the nuclear genome, human mtDNA compacts in an all-or-none fashion, with most nucleoids sitting in an inaccessible, inactive state. TFAM sets the fraction that stays open, acting by nucleation and spreading.

Isaac et al., Nature Structural & Molecular Biology 2024

The lab

Biologists, physicists, chemists and computational scientists in one room

The lab has always been a mix. Stirling trained as a physicist; the people here now came in through biochemistry, neuroscience, chemistry, public health and computation. Methods development rewards that mix — most of our techniques exist because someone with the wrong background asked an obvious question.
